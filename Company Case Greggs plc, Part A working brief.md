# Company Case: Greggs plc, Part A working brief

## 1. The project we chose

**Company:** Greggs plc (LSE: GRG).

**Decision:** the proposal of 30 September 2026 to consolidate in-house manufacturing. It would close manufacturing at Enfield, North Lakes, Pettigrews and Seaham, end manufacturing at Treforest (which stays a distribution centre), reduce ranges at Clydesmill and Manchester, end tinned bread at Gosforth, and source a small number of products from specialist suppliers. Six manufacturing sites would remain: Clydesmill, Gosforth, Balliol, Leeds, Manchester and Derby.

**Headline figures (Q3 Trading Update, 30 Sep 2026):** about 740 roles affected over two and a half years; cash costs of about £60m (about £40m of it capex, the rest disruption costs and redundancy payments); an annual pre-tax cash saving of about £20m, expected across FY2028 and FY2029.

**Who decides:** the proposal comes from the executive team led by CEO Roisin Currie. We assume the board decides after consultation. No final decision had been announced in anything found up to 23 November.

**Alternatives:**

1. Proceed as proposed (including the Derby lines).
2. Close fewer sites or fewer product lines.
3. Keep the current network and meet growth in capacity another way, such as the Derby and Kettering sites where space is reserved.

**Because the decision is open,** we advise as at the date of report. Money already spent by then is sunk. Money still to be spent is not. If the board decides before we submit, we say so in our closing sentences.

**What the data lack (from the proposal):** site-level costs, output and headcount; how the savings and the non-capex part of the £60m split between years; whether Treforest is inside the £60m, £20m and 740 figures; sale or reuse value of closed sites; supplier prices; the 2027 cost effect of the new distribution centres. Where data cannot support an estimate, write one sentence saying why and use a labelled assumption.

**Common errors the brief names, which cost marks:**

- sunk costs counted in the comparison;
- allocated head-office costs treated as avoidable;
- a regression slope on revenue read as a unit variable cost;
- accounting profit used where cash flows are needed, or no discounting over several years;

### 4a. Types of cost

| Concept | Plain meaning | Greggs example to think about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed cost | Stays the same whatever the output (within a range) | Rent or depreciation of a plant, site management |
| Variable cost | Moves with output | Flour, pastry, sausage meat, packaging |
| Mixed (semi-variable) cost | A fixed part plus a part that moves with output | Energy at a plant; a site with a base crew plus overtime |
| Step cost | Fixed over a range, then jumps | Adding a production shift or line |
| Direct vs indirect cost | Traceable to a product or site, or shared | Plant wages vs head office |
| Sunk cost | Already spent and not recoverable, whatever you choose | Past capex on existing plants, and what was already spent on Derby and Kettering by the report date |
| Relevant (differential) cost | Future cash flow that differs between alternatives | Redundancy costs, closure capex, supplier prices, site operating costs that stop |
| Opportunity cost | The benefit you give up by choosing one alternative over another | Using Derby's reserved capacity for the closed sites' output instead of something else; what a closed site could be sold or leased for |
| Avoidable vs unavoidable cost | Stops if you act, or continues anyway | Site-specific wages (avoidable) vs allocated head office (usually unavoidable) |
| Allocated cost | A share of a shared cost spread across sites by a formula | Head-office or central supply chain overhead charged to each plant |
| Committed vs discretionary cost | Locked in by past decisions, or chosen period by period | Derby and Kettering commitments under every alternative |
| Incremental cost | The extra cost of one alternative compared with another | Cost of making the Enfield products at Derby minus Enfield's avoidable cost |

**The three-question test:** is it in the future, does it differ between the alternatives, and does it change cash? Yes to all three means relevant.

### 4b. Decision tools

- **Contribution margin and cost-volume-profit (CVP):** selling price minus variable cost per unit, and how profit moves with volume. Useful for thinking about what happens to margin if volumes grow or product ranges are cut.
- **Cost estimation (Week 2-3):** high-low method (two data points) and regression (best-fit line). The slope is the cost per unit of the activity driver you chose, but only if the driver is a volume of activity. A slope on revenue or on a count like shops trading is not a unit variable cost. Always state the limits: few data points, a changing network, and the segmental data covering only five periods.
- **Relevant costs, make-or-buy and scarce resources (Week 5):** make-or-buy compares the avoidable cost of making in-house with the buying price, and checks the capacity constraint. This is the logic behind alternative 3 (which products stay in-house, and which costs buying them avoids) and the specialist-supplier part of alternative 1.
- **Activity-based costing (ABC):** assigns overhead through cost pools and cost drivers. Useful for questioning whether an allocated charge reflects what a site actually causes.
- **Absorption vs variable costing:** absorption costing spreads fixed overhead into unit costs, which can make a closure look more or less attractive than the avoidable costs really are. Variable costing keeps fixed costs separate. Ask which fixed costs actually go away.
- **Capital investment (Week 5):** cash flows over time, discounted at a stated rate (net present value, NPV). Use cash flows, not accounting profit. Depreciation is not a cash flow. The one-off costs come first and the savings arrive later, so timing matters. Payback and break-even analysis can sit beside NPV, and sensitivity analysis finds the threshold that flips the answer.

### 4c. A note on accounting frameworks

This course is about internal decisions, not reporting. GAAP and IFRS are mostly out of scope. Two places they can show up here: Greggs reports under IFRS, and its impairment test discount rate is the starting point the brief itself suggests for discounting. The reported accounting charge for restructuring may also fall in different years from the cash payments. For the decision, use cash timing.
